# Your report

I analyzed the Reddit stock sentiment dataset with 847 documents. The class distribution from `load_dataset_1` was 423 neutral (`0.0`), 315 negative (`-1.0`), and 109 positive (`1.0`) documents. `check_missing_2` found no missing or empty text, while `check_duplicates_3` found 23 duplicate text rows. The text length analysis in `describe_4` showed a mean length of 147.44 characters, a median of 78, and a maximum of 2737, which suggests that the distribution is strongly right-skewed because a small number of long posts are much longer than most documents. The distribution is shown in [the text length plot](../plots/張秉涵_homework_plot_describe_4.png).

After tokenizing all 847 documents (`tokenize_5`), I built a unigram document-term matrix (`dtm_6`) with 4,346 terms. The matrix had 17,193 non-zero entries out of 3,681,062 total elements, giving 99.53% sparsity. The most frequent terms from `term_frequency_7` were mainly common words such as `the`, `to`, `and`, and `is`, so raw term frequency alone did not reveal many sentiment-specific words. The DTM heatmap (`dtm_heatmap_8`) also contained many number-related tokens because it visualized a positional slice of the feature matrix rather than selecting the most important features. The heatmap is available in [the DTM plot](../plots/張秉涵_homework_plot_dtm_heatmap_8.png).

For feature selection, `variance_filter_9` kept only 35 terms at a threshold of 0.15 and removed 4,311 terms. Most terms with high variance were still common words, although `market` appeared among them, showing that high variance does not necessarily mean that a word is useful for sentiment classification. For positive sentiment, Pearson filtering (`pearson_filter_10`) kept 689 terms and Spearman filtering (`spearman_filter_11`) kept 691 terms. Their rankings were very similar, with terms such as `technology`, `tldr`, `huge`, `nice`, and `potential` appearing near the top. The strongest correlations were still relatively weak, around 0.13–0.16. The feature correlation analysis (`feature_correlation_12`) also showed strong relationships mainly among common high variance words such as `the`, `in`, `of`, and `and`, as shown in [the feature correlation plot](../plots/張秉涵_homework_plot_feature_correlation_12.png).

For frequent pattern mining, I used TF-IDF filtering and Top-K mining on the positive sentiment category (`patterns_13`). The top patterns included `good`, `market`, `silver`, and `economy`, together with more general words such as `just`, `doing`, and `like`. This suggests that the patterns captured some meaningful positive or financial vocabulary, although common conversational words were still present. The three sentiment labels were also one-hot encoded in `binarize_labels_14`, producing a matrix with shape 847*3 and class order `['-1.0', '0.0', '1.0']`.

Finally, I compared PCA, t-SNE, and UMAP. PCA (`reduce_dimensions_16`) explained about 27.0% and 4.99% of the variance in its first two components, but the three sentiment classes were highly overlapped ([PCA plot](../plots/張秉涵_homework_plot_reduce_dimensions_16.png)). t-SNE (`reduce_dimensions_17`) and UMAP (`reduce_dimensions_18`) revealed more local groups, but the sentiment labels were still mixed instead of forming separate clusters ([t-SNE plot](../plots/張秉涵_homework_plot_reduce_dimensions_17.png), [UMAP plot](../plots/張秉涵_homework_plot_reduce_dimensions_18.png)). Cosine similarity also showed that vocabulary similarity does not directly represent sentiment, two negative documents had a similarity of 0.0 (`cosine_similarity_19`), but a negative and a positive document had a similarity of 0.0585 (`cosine_similarity_20`). This indicates that the unigram count representation mainly captures shared words rather than sentiment meaning.
